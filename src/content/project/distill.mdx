<Lede>
For any paper, blog post or repo there is a minimum curriculum that takes you from zero to really
understanding it. Distill tries to write that curriculum: it finds the bare prerequisites, builds a
dependency graph up from them, and ends at the original source, with lessons to read and exercises to run
along the way.
</Lede>

## Why I built it

It started with a need: to learn things deeply, not superficially. I would find a topic on X or in a
technical blog and want to understand it, really understand it, not just know what it says.

Doing that by hand means going down the rabbit hole: following each thing you don't know back to what it
rests on, until you hit ground you already stand on. My hypothesis is that this dig can be done for you.
Extract the essential prerequisites, order them into a dependency graph, and you have the shortest path
from the bare minimum to a fair understanding of the source.

I let LLMs do the writing because I think they are already good enough to teach. What they don't do on
their own is start in the right place: they teach from where you are, not from where the topic needs you
to begin. That is why Distill is mostly a harness around the model, keeping it on the curriculum, and I
think some harness like it will almost always be needed.

I had an adult learner in mind: someone already technical, reading a paper or a technical blog, who has to
take the topic apart into a curriculum before they can learn it. I think the idea is more general than
that, but the picture I keep coming back to is Brilliant for adults: for people who are already technical
and hungry for more.

## How it works

**Blueprint.** Distill reads the whole source and writes a curriculum: which modules depend on which, a
contract for every exercise (what is provided and what you write), the key excerpts, and how each one is
validated.

**Generate.** Each module is a multi-turn conversation with the full source. The model writes the lesson
first, then the exercises one at a time. Solutions are executed between turns, so the real output of
exercise 1 feeds the prompt for exercise 2. Modules generate in parallel.

**Review.** Pre-flight checks (syntax, leftover TODOs, output patterns) catch structural problems, an LLM
review checks the teaching and the contracts, and failed modules are generated again.

Lessons are teaching documents of 3,000 to 10,000 words, not summaries. Exercises are `.py` files with TODO
blocks, docstrings and a `__main__` test harness, with solutions included.

## Open questions

**Figures.** I still don't trust LLMs to make good teaching images. Distill draws with Excalidraw and
ASCII, and that may have been the wrong call; plain LaTeX might do better. That remains to be tested.

**Reading I haven't done.** I built this from intuition. Next would be the research on how people learn,
the work on reinforcement learning for education, and what Khan Academy has written about these
principles.
